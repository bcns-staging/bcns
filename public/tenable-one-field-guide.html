Read this first

What the job description is actually buying, how to use this set, and the one sentence that has to be true at the end of nine months.

OrientationAll scopes

This is not a "deploy a scanner" role. It is a consulting engagement disguised as an engineering role: you arrive at a global data center operator, design an exposure management program that spans server racks and chiller controllers, build it, prove it produces numbers an executive will trust, and then leave it running in somebody else's hands. The nine-month duration is the tell — the deliverable is a program, not a platform.

SHEET01

Vulnerability — exposure management

The conceptual shift the platform is built around, and why the distinction matters commercially as well as technically.

Fundamentals

1.1 What changed, and why

Classic vulnerability management is a loop around a finding: scan a subnet, produce CVEs, sort by CVSS, send a spreadsheet to whoever owns the box, repeat monthly. It fails in three predictable ways — it only sees what you already knew to scan, it treats a lab VM and a domain controller identically, and it produces more critical findings than any team can ever close.

Exposure management is a loop around an asset and its context. The unit of work is not "CVE-2024-XXXX on 10.4.2.9" but "this internet-reachable jump host, which has a path to the identity tier, has a weakness an attacker is actively using." Vulnerabilities are one input; misconfigurations, excessive identity privilege, unmanaged external assets and network reachability are the others.

Asset-centric

One record per real thing, enriched from every sensor and from the CMDB — not one record per scan result.

Context-weighted

Business criticality and exploitability shift priority, so a "medium" on a control system can outrank a "critical" on a test box.

Path-aware

Attack path analysis asks what a weakness enables, not just how bad it scores in isolation.

Continuous

Discovery and assessment run constantly; the quarterly scan cycle is the thing being replaced.

Fig 1.1 — The unit of work changes from the finding to the asset. Same sensors, same CVEs; a completely different conversation with the team that has to act.

1.2 CTEM — the operating loop underneath

Gartner's Continuous Threat Exposure Management framing is the vocabulary your stakeholders will have read. Learn the five stages because they map cleanly onto your work plan and give you a defensible structure for the roadmap:

  1. Scoping — decide what is in the program. For this engagement: which sites, which VLANs, which facility systems, which cloud accounts. This is where OT gets included or quietly dropped.
  2. Discovery — find assets and their weaknesses. Sensors, connectors, CMDB reconciliation.
  3. Prioritization — rank by exploitability plus business impact, not raw severity.
  4. Validation — confirm the exposure is real and reachable, and that the fix worked. Attack path analysis and re-scan verification live here.
  5. Mobilization — get humans to act. Workflows, SLAs, ownership, tickets. This is where most programs die, and where the JD spends most of its "Operationalization" bullets.
Framing that landsStages 1–4 are a tooling problem and you can finish them in a quarter. Stage 5 is an organisational problem and it is the reason the contract is nine months long. Say that out loud early — it sets expectations and it demonstrates you have done this before.
Fig 1.2 — The CTEM loop. Say out loud in week one which stage you think the customer is stuck at; it is almost always stage 5.

1.3 Where the data center context bites

  • Availability outranks confidentiality. In a colo, downtime is a contractual penalty. Any control you propose gets evaluated against "could this cause an outage" first. Your scanning design must lead with blast-radius reasoning.
  • Split ownership. IT owns servers; Facilities owns the plant; the colo customer may own gear inside your building that you cannot touch. Scope boundaries need to be written down before the first scan, and demarcation disputes are the most common source of program stall.
  • Long-lived, unpatchable assets. A BMS controller commissioned in 2013 with a firmware update that requires a vendor engineer on site is not going to be remediated in 30 days. Your operating model needs a credible mitigation and exception track, or the OT scope becomes a permanent red number on the dashboard that everyone learns to ignore.
  • Compliance overlay. Expect SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI (if customers process card data in the space), and increasingly NIS2/NERC-adjacent expectations depending on geography. These drive evidence requirements — scan coverage proof, remediation timelines, exception approvals — so build reporting that produces audit evidence as a by-product.
SHEET02

The prioritization math

CVSS, VPR, EPSS, ACR, AES and the Cyber Exposure Score — what each one measures, and which argument to use with which audience.

FundamentalsReporting

Every serious conversation in this role eventually becomes an argument about what to fix first. If you can explain these six numbers cleanly — including their weaknesses — you will win most of those arguments.

ScoreMeasuresRangeUse it forIts blind spot
CVSSTheoretical severity of the flaw itself0–10Compliance language, audit mapping, vendor SLAsStatic, no threat context; over half the CVE corpus is "High" or "Critical," so it cannot prioritise
EPSSProbability the CVE is exploited in the wild in the next 30 days0–1Sanity-checking, cross-vendor argumentsProbabilistic and volatile; says nothing about your environment
VPRTenable's threat-informed severity: exploit availability, exploit maturity, chatter, age, plus CVSS0–10The default lens for "which vulnerability matters"Still vulnerability-centric — ignores whose asset it is
ACRAsset Criticality Rating — how much the asset matters to the business1–10Injecting business context; the single highest-leverage tuning you doDefaults are guesses. Uncurated ACR silently ruins everything downstream
AESAsset Exposure Score — combines the asset's vulnerabilities (VPR) with its ACR0–1000Ranking assets for remediation, assigning work to ownersOnly as good as the ACR and the scan coverage feeding it
CESCyber Exposure Score — aggregate AES across a scope, weighted0–1000Executive trending, business-unit comparison, benchmarkingMoves when coverage changes, not just when risk changes — explain this before the first board slide
The trap everyone falls intoYou onboard 4,000 new assets in month three. CES jumps. The CISO asks why security got worse. It did not — visibility improved. Publish a coverage line on every exposure chart so score movement is always read against asset count, and annotate onboarding events on the trend line from day one.
Fig 2.1 — Each layer adds context the layer below cannot see. The amber block is the only input you own: uncurated ACR silently corrupts everything above it.

Turning scores into a decision

Scores rank; they do not decide. The decision rule you will write into the operating model looks more like this, and it is deliberately readable by a non-specialist:

# Remediation priority — plain-language rule
P1  exploited-in-the-wild  AND  asset is internet-facing or Tier-0 identity
P2  VPR >= 9.0             AND  ACR >= 8   # crown jewels, weaponised bug
P3  VPR >= 7.0             AND  ACR >= 5
P4  everything else that is not suppressed
--  OT assets never auto-assign; they route to the facilities triage queue

Exposure signals and attack paths

The platform's differentiator is combining domains. Exposure signals surface toxic combinations — a vulnerable host that also holds a privileged credential that also has a route to a critical system. Attack path analysis renders those relationships as a graph mapped to MITRE ATT&CK and identifies choke points: single nodes that appear across many paths.

Fig 2.2 — Why a choke point is the most persuasive slide you own: it converts an unbounded backlog into a finite list a team will actually finish.
Why choke points sell"Fix these six things and you break 400 attack paths" is the most persuasive slide in the platform, because it converts a remediation ask into a small, finite list. Build your first executive readout around a choke-point story rather than a CVE count.

ACR curation — do this early or pay later

  • Derive criticality from something that already exists and is maintained: the CMDB's business-service mapping, the DR tier, or the customer's own site-criticality register. Do not invent a parallel taxonomy.
  • Agree a small number of bands (e.g. 10 = life-safety and power/cooling control, 9 = Tier-0 identity, 7 = revenue-bearing production, 4 = internal supporting, 2 = lab/dev) and write down the definition of each.
  • Automate the assignment by tag so it survives asset churn, and re-run it on a schedule. Manual per-asset ACR does not survive contact with 30,000 assets.
  • Get the band definitions signed off by someone who owns budget. ACR is where security judgement and business judgement meet, and it should not be your opinion alone.

The mental modelThree layers. Sensors collect (scanners, agents, OT appliances, collectors, connectors). Modules assess and score within a domain. The exposure layer unifies, scores across domains, and reports. Most deployment failures are sensor-layer failures — coverage gaps and bad credentials — that only become visible as nonsense in the top layer.

3.2 The fork: Vulnerability Management or Security Center?

This is the first real architecture decision, it is expensive to reverse, and interviewers ask about it because it separates people who have deployed from people who have read the datasheet.

Choose VM (cloud) when—

Sites are globally distributed, ops headcount is thin, you want ASM/Web App/Identity and attack paths natively, and outbound 443 from each site is achievable.

Choose Security Center when—

Data residency or contractual terms forbid findings leaving the estate, air-gapped enclaves exist, or a regulator requires on-prem retention.

Hybrid is normal

SC for the restricted enclave, VM everywhere else, both surfaced in the exposure layer via connector. Say "hybrid" and explain the seam — do not pretend one console fits all.

The seam to watch

Duplicate asset records across consoles. Reconciliation keys and tag parity have to be designed before the second console exists, not after.

In a global colo footprint the default is VM, because distributed sites plus a small ops team is exactly the case SaaS wins. Argue for Security Center only where a specific written requirement forces it — and make the customer show you the requirement.

SHEET04

Sensor placement board

The core skill of this role: matching a collection method to a zone. Work the board, then read the reasoning.

DesignDesignBlast radius

4.1 The five ways Tenable One sees an asset

Active scan

Nessus connects to the target. Deepest data when credentialed. Generates traffic and load — the method with real blast radius.

Agent

Assessment runs on the host, reports out. No credentials in flight, works for roaming and off-network hosts, but cannot see network-visible issues like exposed services or expired TLS.

Passive listening

Traffic mirrored to a sensor; assets and weaknesses inferred from what they say. Zero risk to the target, shallower detail, only sees what talks.

Protocol-native query

OT active query: read-only requests in the device's own language (e.g. a PLC state read) rather than generic probes. Deep OT detail with far less risk than a Nessus scan.

API / connector

Cloud accounts, CMDB, EDR, third-party scanners. No packets near the asset at all. Perfect for environments you cannot touch.

Fig 4.1 — Every placement decision is this trade. Move down and right wherever you can; never sit top-left.

4.2 Work the board

Ten zones from a realistic global colo estate. Pick the primary collection method for each. Verdicts and reasoning appear as you go.

4.3 Placement rules worth memorising

  • Scanner goes where the traffic should stay. One scanner per site or per segment group, assessing locally and shipping only results upstream. Never scan across a WAN link if you can avoid it — you burn bandwidth, inflate scan windows, and produce timeouts that look like vulnerabilities.
  • Prefer outbound-only. Linked scanners initiate to the console over 443; nothing needs to be opened inbound to a colo cage. This single fact resolves most firewall objections.
  • Agents for anything that moves or is hard to credential; network scans for anything you cannot install software on — appliances, printers, network gear, storage arrays, embedded devices.
  • Both, deliberately, for servers. Agent for depth and reliability, periodic credentialed network scan for the outside-in view. Plan the deduplication before you turn both on.
  • Never point a general-purpose scanner at Level 0–2. That is the OT appliance's job, and even then you start passive.
  • Size for the window, not the asset count. The question is never "how many assets" — it is "how many assets can I finish inside the change window at an acceptable concurrency."
Blast-radius checklist before any new scan target Is it embedded or safety-related? · Does the vendor warranty prohibit scanning? · Is it single-homed on a fragile stack? · Has it ever fallen over during a scan before? · Is there a maintenance window and a rollback owner? · Have you disabled dangerous plugin families and capped concurrency? — If you cannot answer all six, you are not ready to launch.

5.2 Connectivity you must confirm in discovery

  • Outbound 443 from each scanner and appliance to the Tenable cloud, including through any proxy. Get the required destination list from current documentation and give the network team FQDNs, not IPs — cloud endpoints move.
  • Proxy behaviour: authenticated proxies, TLS inspection and certificate pinning break scanner linking in that order of frequency. Ask specifically whether TLS is intercepted.
  • Scanner — target reachability: which VLANs the scanner may source traffic into, and whether a firewall sits between. A firewall in path silently changes results — dropped SYNs read as closed ports, rate limits read as unresponsive hosts.
  • Credential path: scanner to vault (CyberArk, Delinea, Azure Key Vault), and scanner to target on 22/445/5985.
  • Time sync and DNS. Bad NTP breaks certificate validation and makes scan timestamps unusable for SLA reporting; bad reverse DNS wrecks asset identity.
  • Bandwidth budget per site. Agree a ceiling with the network team and configure to it. In a colo, transit may be metered.

5.3 Sizing and scan windows

Use a simple, defensible model rather than a vendor rule of thumb, and show your working:

# Rough capacity check per scanner
hosts_per_window = (window_hours * 3600) / avg_seconds_per_host * concurrency_factor

# credentialed Windows/Linux server, full plugin set: 90–240s typical
# discovery-only sweep:                                 1–5s
# network appliance, SNMP/SSH:                          30–90s

Then: split by tag into staggered jobs, never one monolithic /16 scan.
Then: leave 30% headroom for retries, credential failures and growth.
Fig 5.2 — Split the schedule by purpose, not by subnet. Daily cheap discovery keeps inventory honest; expensive assessment is staggered so no single night is overloaded.
Design move that saves the programRun a fast unauthenticated discovery sweep daily and a deep credentialed assessment weekly, in separate policies. Discovery keeps inventory honest and catches rogue assets within a day; the deep scan carries the expensive plugins and fits inside a real change window. Mixing both into one nightly job is the most common cause of scans that never finish.

5.4 Segmentation questions for the design workshop

  • Where are the IT/OT boundaries, and is there a formal DMZ between them or a flat path someone will discover during discovery?
  • Which segments are customer-owned in the colo halls, and what is the written demarcation? Scanning a colo tenant's equipment without authorisation is a contractual and potentially legal problem — get scope in writing.
  • Is out-of-band management (iDRAC/iLO/IPMI, serial concentrators, smart PDUs) in scope? It usually should be — it is the highest-value, least-scanned network in the building.
  • Do the sites share address space? Overlapping RFC1918 ranges across sites are common and they destroy IP-based asset identity. Plan for scanner-scoped identity and non-IP correlation keys.
  • Where can you get a SPAN or TAP, and who owns the switch? This determines OT coverage more than any other factor.

6.3 Passive versus active — the answer they want to hear

Tenable's own guidance is a layered one: passive monitoring across all levels for continuous visibility, with active methods reserved for the upper levels where the assets are conventional computers. Beneath that, use protocol-native read-only queries rather than general scanning, and only with operations agreement.

MethodRisk to plantData depthWhere it belongs
Passive network detectionNone — mirrored traffic onlyAssets, protocols, conversations, some firmware and version detailEverywhere, always. This is your baseline.
OT active query (protocol-native)Low, but non-zeroFirmware, backplane modules, PLC run state, precise model dataLevel 1–2, enabled selectively, per query type, agreed with operations

How the OT module is put togetherAn ICP appliance per site analyses traffic from a SPAN, mirror or TAP, and runs both passive detection and the active query engine. Sensors — up to roughly one per managed switch — sit on segments the appliance cannot see, capture and compress traffic back to the appliance, and can themselves be enabled to issue active queries into their local segments. The appliance wants a monitoring interface fed by SPAN and a separate management/query interface, and port separation is supported when the operations team insists the monitoring path stays passive-only.
Fig 6.2 — Where the OT dependency really sits: SPAN port access. It is a network-engineering request, it takes weeks, and it gates all OT visibility — so raise it in week one.
Never say this"Active scanning is safe, it's read-only." Tenable itself will not guarantee active scanning cannot disturb operation, some vendor warranties explicitly prohibit scanning and agent installation, and every OT engineer in the room has a story about a device that fell over. The credible sentence is: "We start passive, we prove value with passive, and every active step is tested and agreed before it runs in production."

Rules of engagement — write these down and get them signed

  • Named operations approver per site, plus a documented stop authority: anyone in the control room can halt collection, no questions asked.
  • Change-window alignment with facilities maintenance calendars — not the IT change calendar; they are different, and facilities' one usually wins.
  • Written device exclusion list: fire/life safety, anything under active vendor warranty dispute, anything in commissioning.
  • Lab or spare-unit validation before enabling any new query type against a device family.
  • Rollback and escalation path, including who calls the vendor if a controller misbehaves during a collection window.
  • Recorded baseline of normal behaviour first, so anomalies after your changes can be attributed honestly.

Remediation reality in OT

Most OT findings will not be patched this quarter, and pretending otherwise destroys your credibility with facilities. Design the OT track around compensating controls — segmentation and firewall rule tightening, removing default credentials, disabling unused services and remote-access paths, restricting vendor access, and monitoring — with patching batched into scheduled maintenance shutdowns. Report OT posture on a separate track with its own targets, so a five-year-old unpatchable controller does not sit permanently red on the enterprise dashboard.

Say this in the interview"In OT my first deliverable isn't a vulnerability report, it's an accurate asset inventory and a conversation with facilities about what we can safely touch. Passive gets me the inventory in weeks with zero operational risk, and it buys the trust I need before I ask to query anything."
SHEET07

Asset identity & tagging

The unglamorous design decision that determines whether every dashboard, SLA and report you build later is trustworthy.

Data modelReporting

Ask a deployment engineer what went wrong on their last project and the answer is almost always duplicate or unattributed assets. Exposure scores are asset-weighted; if the asset record is wrong, every number above it is wrong, and stakeholders will find the error before you do.

7.1 Why duplicates happen

  • The same host seen by an agent, a credentialed scan and a cloud connector, with three different identifiers.
  • DHCP churn and IP reuse across sites with overlapping RFC1918 space.
  • Ephemeral cloud instances and containers that live for hours.
  • Cloned VMs sharing a machine identifier, or a golden image baked with an agent already linked.
  • Devices with multiple NICs, or NAT between scanner and target.
CountermeasuresBake agents into images unlinked and link at first boot. Set aging rules so ephemeral assets expire rather than accumulate. Prefer strong identifiers — agent UUID, cloud instance ID, CMDB correlation ID — over IP and hostname. Reconcile against the CMDB continuously and review the "no CMDB match" queue weekly; that queue is also your rogue-asset detector.
Fig 7.1 — Asset identity is the hinge of the whole program. Get the matching keys wrong and every number above it is quietly false.

7.2 Tag taxonomy — design it once, on purpose

Tags are how you slice exposure, route remediation, scope scans and drive reporting. Prefer dynamic, rule-based tags sourced from authoritative data over static lists that rot. A workable taxonomy has a small number of orthogonal dimensions — build one below and see what it unlocks.

7.3 Rules of good tagging

  • Every dimension answers a question someone will ask. If no report or workflow needs it, do not create it.
  • Source from truth, not typing. Derive tags from CMDB fields, cloud tags, AD OUs, subnet ranges or naming conventions — and fix the source when it is wrong rather than patching the tag.
  • Owner is mandatory. An asset with no remediation owner is not a finding, it is an unassigned task that will age forever. Make "untagged owner" a tracked metric with a target of zero.
  • Keep the vocabulary controlled. prod, Prod and PRD as three tags is how a taxonomy dies. Document allowed values.
  • Design ACR assignment as a tag consequence, so criticality is applied consistently and re-applied automatically to new assets.
  • Version it. Tag schema changes silently redraw every dashboard; treat schema edits as change-managed and note them on trend charts.

8.2 Credentialed scanning — the highest-leverage work

Uncredentialed scanning tells you a host exists and which ports answer. Credentialed scanning tells you what is actually installed and patched, and it is the difference between a program people believe and a program people argue with. Expect to spend more time on credentials than on anything else in the build.

Windows

Domain account, admin rights on target, SMB and remote registry reachable, admin shares present. Group Policy that disables remote registry is the classic silent killer.

Linux/Unix

SSH with key auth, a defined privilege escalation method, and a sudoers entry that actually permits the commands the plugins run.

Network devices

SSH or SNMPv3 with authPriv. Community strings still lurking on facility gear are themselves a finding.

Vault-backed

Integrate with CyberArk/Delinea rather than storing credentials in scan policies. Rotation then never breaks scanning, and audit is happy.

Least privilege realityA scanning account is a privileged account across your whole estate — it is exactly what an attacker wants. Constrain it: dedicated account, no interactive logon, source-restricted to scanner IPs, vault-managed rotation, alerting on use from anywhere else. Being able to discuss this unprompted marks you as senior.
Fig 8.1 — The credential path, and every place it silently fails. This is where most of your build time actually goes.

8.3 Deploying agents at scale

  • Ship via the tooling that already exists — SCCM/Intune, Ansible, Chef, Jamf, a golden image — never by hand.
  • Protect the linking key like a credential; it lets anything join your tenant.
  • Plan agent groups to mirror your tag dimensions so scan targeting and reporting align.
  • Watch for clone collisions from images baked with a linked agent; unlink before sealing.
  • Monitor agent health as a first-class metric: linked, last-seen, plugin-set age. A silent agent is a coverage gap that looks like a clean asset.
  • Agree an update strategy — staggered rings, not all-at-once across a global estate.
Fig 8.2 — Report the deltas explicitly. “110 assets unexplained” is the honest number, and driving it to zero is a legitimate month-two deliverable.

8.4 Triage runbook: the three failures

Failure 1 — "The scan says the host is fine" (silent credential failure)

A credentialed scan that fails authentication still completes, still reports, and produces a short, clean-looking result. This is the single most dangerous data-quality failure in vulnerability management because it manufactures false confidence.

  1. Check the authentication-success plugin output per host, not the scan status.
  2. Treat credential failure as a monitored metric with a target (e.g. —98% of in-scope hosts authenticating) and alert on regression.
  3. Common causes: expired or rotated password, account lockout from parallel scans, remote registry disabled, UAC remote restrictions, missing sudo entry, SSH key not distributed, host-based firewall, or a jump in host count after new builds land without the account provisioned.
  4. Fix at source — bake the scanning account into build automation so new hosts are scannable on day one.
Failure 2 — Coverage gaps you cannot see

You can only report on assets you know exist. Prove coverage by triangulating independent sources rather than trusting the platform's own asset count.

  • Reconcile against DHCP leases, ARP/CAM tables from the network team, EDR console, CMDB, cloud inventory APIs and the identity directory.
  • Report the delta explicitly: "CMDB has 12,400 assets, we assess 11,910, 380 are excluded by policy, 110 are unexplained." That last number is the one that matters, and driving it to zero is a legitimate month-two deliverable.
  • Watch for whole subnets missing because a scanner cannot route to them, an exclusion list is stale, or a site was never added after a colo expansion.
Failure 3 — False positives and the credibility spiral

One badly-handled false positive can cost you an entire team's cooperation. Backported security patches on enterprise Linux are the classic case: version-based detection flags a package that the distributor already fixed.

  • Prefer credentialed and local-package checks over banner-grabbing detection wherever possible.
  • Give owners a formal dispute path with a service commitment — you will investigate within N days and respond with evidence either way.
  • Distinguish a true false positive from an accepted risk. Suppress the former, document the latter as an exception with an expiry date.
  • Track suppression volume. A rising suppression rate is either a detection-quality problem or a governance problem, and both need surfacing.
SHEET09

Integrations

ServiceNow, SIEM, identity and cloud. Where the platform stops being a scanner and starts being a program.

OperationalizationBuild

9.1 ServiceNow — CMDB and ITSM are two different projects

Say this precisely, because conflating them is a common junior mistake:

CMDB direction (in)

ServiceNow gives you business context: owner, service, criticality, environment, location. This is what makes tagging and ACR meaningful.

ITSM direction (out)

Tenable creates and updates incidents, changes or vulnerability response records. This is what makes remediation trackable.

Correlation is the hard part

Agree the matching key up front — serial, cloud instance ID, FQDN, MAC. Expect a real reconciliation exercise before either direction is trustworthy.

Do not flood

One ticket per finding per host at enterprise scale is tens of thousands of tickets and instant revolt.

Ticketing design that survivesGroup by owner + remediation action, not by finding. "Patch group X to April cumulative update, 240 hosts, closes 1,900 findings" is one actionable ticket. Aim for tickets a team can complete in a sprint, with the finding detail attached rather than exploded. Then set the auto-close rule on verified re-scan, not on the assignee's say-so.

9.3 Order of operations

Sequence matters. Build integrations in the order that makes the next one easier:

  1. Identity/SSO first — you need scoped roles before other teams touch the console.
  2. CMDB in — context before automation, or you will automate tickets to the wrong owners at scale.
  3. Cloud connectors — cheap coverage wins that need no field work.
  4. ITSM out, in pilot — one business unit, manual approval on ticket creation, tune the grouping rules against real feedback.
  5. SIEM — once findings are prioritised well enough to be worth alerting on.
  6. Everything else — after the operating model exists, so integrations serve a workflow rather than inventing one.
SHEET10

The operating model

SLAs, exceptions, escalation and ownership. The part that outlives you.

Operationalization

A vulnerability management standard is a short document that answers four questions: who fixes what, how fast, what happens when they cannot, and who is told when they do not. Everything else is commentary. Write it in weeks 4–8, socialise it, and get it signed by someone senior enough to enforce it.

10.1 Build the SLA matrix

Two axes — how dangerous the weakness is, how much the asset matters. Adjust the sliders and the matrix recalculates. Aim for numbers the customer can actually hit; an aspirational SLA that is missed 70% of the time is worse than a modest one that is met.

10.2 Exceptions and risk acceptance

The exception process is the pressure-release valve. Without one, teams simply ignore findings and your metrics become fiction. With a rigorous one, your dashboard stays honest.

  • Every exception has an expiry. 90 days by default, one year maximum, no permanent exceptions ever.
  • Every exception names a compensating control and the residual risk in a sentence a non-specialist understands.
  • Approval scales with risk. Low findings: asset owner. High: security lead. Critical or Tier-0: CISO or a named risk committee.
  • Exceptions are visible. Publish the count and the aging alongside remediation metrics — a growing exception register is a signal, not an embarrassment.
  • OT gets its own template with mitigation language, since "patch in 30 days" is frequently not physically possible.
Fig 10.1 — Three exits, and only one of them is silent failure. Every finding must end in a verified fix, a documented exception, or an escalation with a name on it.

Daily

Weekly

Monthly

Quarterly

Work queue. What is assigned to me, due when, with the fix action. No scores, no trends — just the next task and the evidence to do it.

11.3 Writing the executive page

  1. Lead with the direction of travel and one sentence of interpretation — "exposure down 18% this quarter, driven by the identity remediation push; coverage up 6% so the improvement is real."
  2. Convert risk into consequence. "Three internet-facing systems have weaknesses under active exploitation" beats "417 critical vulnerabilities."
  3. Show the ask. Every executive readout should end with a decision you need from them: funding, a mandate, an accepted risk, or an escalation.
  4. Report OT separately with its own targets and its mitigation narrative.
  5. Annotate the chart when scope changes. Unexplained jumps destroy trust in the whole program.
Say this in the interview"I never present an exposure score without the coverage line next to it. Otherwise the first time we onboard a new site, the score jumps and I'm explaining statistics instead of risk."

12.2 API patterns that matter

Tenable exposes REST APIs across the products, with pyTenable as the maintained Python client. Four practical rules cover most of what you will build:

  • Use export endpoints, not paginated list endpoints, for bulk data. Exports are asynchronous — request, poll for chunk readiness, download chunks. This is the only pattern that scales to a full estate.
  • Respect rate limits and implement backoff. Retry with exponential delay on 429 and 5xx, and make jobs idempotent so a retry cannot double-create tickets.
  • Filter server-side. Pull findings since a timestamp, above a severity, within a tag — never pull everything and filter locally.
  • Keys are credentials. Store in a vault, scope to a dedicated service account with least privilege, rotate on a schedule, and never in a repo or a runbook screenshot.
# Export-and-poll: the pattern behind almost every automation you will write
1. POST /vulns/export        # filters: since, severity, tag, state
2. GET  /vulns/export/{uuid}/status   # poll until chunks available
3. GET  /vulns/export/{uuid}/chunks/{n}
4. transform -> group by (owner_tag, fix_action)
5. upsert into ITSM by deterministic external_id  # idempotent
6. write run summary to log + alert on zero-record runs

# Confirm current endpoint paths against developer docs — they do change.

12.3 Automation you will hand over

  • Source control from the first script. No exceptions, no "temporary" scripts on a laptop.
  • Configuration in files, not hard-coded — the customer will change tags, thresholds and endpoints after you leave.
  • Structured logging and a health signal. Silent-failure automation is worse than no automation, because people trust it.
  • A README that states what it does, what it needs, how to run it manually, and how it fails. Assume the reader has never met you.
  • Run it somewhere the customer owns — their scheduler, their runner, their service account. Not yours.

Handoff & enablement

The actual deliverable. Design for it from week one, not from month eight.

Handoff

A nine-month engagement has one failure mode above all others: a working platform that nobody can operate. Every design decision should be checked against the question "can their team run this without me?" — and where the answer is no, the simpler design usually wins.

13.2 Enablement that works

  • Role-based, not tool-tour. Separate sessions for analysts (triage and investigation), engineers (platform operation), asset owners (how to read your ticket and dispute a finding), and executives (how to read the dashboard).
  • Shadow then reverse-shadow. They watch you run the monthly cycle twice, then you watch them run it twice while you sit on your hands.
  • Record the sessions. Staff turn over; the recording outlives the class.
  • Hand over live work, early. Give the customer's analysts the triage queue in month five, not month nine. The problems surface while you are still there to fix them.
  • Run a handover dry-run a month before you leave: you take a week off from the program and see what breaks.
