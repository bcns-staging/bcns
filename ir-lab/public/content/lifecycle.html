Every doctrine note attached to every choice in every scenario ties back to one of four phases defined in NIST Special Publication 800-61, the U.S. government's standard incident-handling framework. It's not a checklist you follow once — it's a cycle: what you learn in the last phase feeds back into the first phase for the next incident.

The NIST SP 800-61 lifecycle
1. Preparation
policies, tooling, tabletop exercises, pre-authorized containment authority
→
2. Detection & Analysis
confirm scope before acting, but don't let confirming replace acting
→
3. Containment, Eradication & Recovery
stop the bleeding, confirm the door is actually closed, restore from verified-clean state
→
4. Post-Incident Activity
blameless postmortem, feed lessons back into Preparation

Why this shows up in every scenario

  • Preparation is almost always invisible in the moment — it's the pre-authorized decision-making authority, the retained IR firm on speed-dial, the written callback-verification policy that makes a stressed employee's judgment call unnecessary. Every scenario has at least one ending that's worse specifically because something wasn't prepared in advance.
  • Detection & Analysis is a genuine trap: waiting for full certainty before acting is itself a costly decision, since the incident keeps developing while you build confidence. Good response confirms enough to act, then keeps confirming in parallel with acting.
  • Containment, Eradication & Recovery is where most of a scenario's decision points live — and where "fast" and "correct" are both necessary, not a tradeoff to pick one of. Stopping the immediate threat without confirming the door is actually closed (a clean backup, a fully-scoped mailbox compromise, a properly preserved chain of custody) just delays the same incident.
  • Post-Incident Activity is the phase real organizations skip most often under time pressure, and the one every scenario's best ending explicitly includes: a documented policy change, a filed civil claim, a completed forensic sweep — something that makes the next incident's Preparation phase stronger because of what was learned here.
